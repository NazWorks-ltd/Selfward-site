Most habit apps count streaks. Selfward builds identity. It helps you decide who you're becoming, stack tiny habits onto the things you already do, and it keeps you from taking on too much, too soon.

Why it works

Built on how habits
actually form.

Every part of Selfward comes from decades of behavior research. Here's the short version. Tap + for the longer one.

  • I want to run more. I am a runner.

    Identity first

    Start with who, not what.

    Goals end at a finish line. Identity keeps going. Each time you show up, it's a vote for the person you're becoming.

    Identity first

    Start with who, not what.

    Most people set outcome goals, like "run a 10K" or "read 20 books". When the goal is reached or missed, the behavior usually stops. Identity-based habits flip the order. You decide who you're becoming ("I am a runner") and choose habits that prove it.

    Research on identity and motivation backs this up. In one study, asking people to be a voter raised turnout more than asking them to vote. Framing a behavior as part of who you are makes it matter more.

    In Selfward: setup starts with "Who do you want to become?" Your "I am…" line sits at the top of Today, on your map and in every reminder.

    Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck (2011), PNAS. Oyserman (2009), identity-based motivation. Clear (2018), Atomic Habits.

  • After I pour my coffee I will put on my shoes

    Habit stacking

    Stack on what you already do.

    "After I ___, I will ___." Tying a new habit to an existing routine gives it a clear trigger, so you don't have to remember it.

    Habit stacking

    Stack on what you already do.

    An if-then plan ("when X happens, I'll do Y") is called an implementation intention. It's one of the best-studied tools in behavior change. In a review of 94 studies, people who made if-then plans followed through much more often than people who only set a goal.

    Habit stacking uses a routine you already do every day as the "if". Your morning coffee already happens without fail, so it's a reliable cue for something new.

    In Selfward: you list the routines you never skip, and every new habit has to be paired with one of them, or with the habit below it.

    Gollwitzer (1999), American Psychologist. Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology.

  • 2:00

    Start tiny

    Two minutes is plenty.

    Make it so small you can't say no. Early on, getting started matters more than how much you do.

    Start tiny

    Two minutes is plenty.

    Habits form through repetition in a steady context, not through intensity. A tiny version you do every day builds the cue-to-action link faster than an ambitious version you skip half the time.

    "Put on my running shoes" feels almost too easy. That's the point. Once starting is automatic, you can make it bigger.

    In Selfward: the habit screen prompts "Make it so small you can't say no." When a habit gets hit or miss, the weekly check-up suggests Make it smaller.

    Fogg (2019), Tiny Habits. Clear (2018), the two-minute rule. Lally et al. (2010).

  • Less friction

    Environment design

    Make it obvious and easy.

    Small bits of friction decide more than willpower does. Set things up ahead of time so the easy path is the right one.

    Environment design

    Make it obvious and easy.

    Research on habits shows that people who seem to have lots of self-control mostly have fewer temptations and less friction. Their surroundings do the work. Moving an obstacle a few steps away, or putting a cue in plain sight, changes behavior far more than trying harder.

    Shoes by the coffee maker. A book on your pillow. A full water glass by the sink.

    In Selfward: when a habit starts, a setup screen opens: "Now make it easy." It suggests ideas matched to your habit and your cue.

    Wood (2019), Good Habits, Bad Habits. Wood & Neal (2007), Psychological Review.

  • One miss keeps your run.

    Forgiveness

    Never miss twice.

    Missing one day doesn't undo your progress. What counts is getting back on track right away.

    Forgiveness

    Never miss twice.

    In the best-known study of real-world habit formation, missing a single chance to do the behavior did not noticeably hurt how the habit formed. All-or-nothing streaks are what make people quit. One bad day turns into "I've blown it".

    In Selfward: one missed day keeps your run, and two in a row starts it over. After a miss, Today gently reminds you: "Missed yesterday. Never miss twice." Days you've set as off are never counted as misses.

    Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010), European Journal of Social Psychology.

  • 66 days

    Automaticity

    A habit is when you stop thinking.

    On average, a new behavior took about 66 days to feel automatic. The range was huge, so Selfward lets you decide when a habit is locked in.

    Automaticity

    A habit is when you stop thinking.

    Forget "21 days to form a habit". When researchers tracked people building new daily habits, automaticity rose quickly at first and then leveled off. It took a median of 66 days to reach the plateau, with a range from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the behavior.

    The real finish line isn't a day count. It's the moment you do the thing without deciding to.

    In Selfward: you choose a target of 7, 14, 21, 30 or 66 days. When you reach it, Selfward asks, "Does it happen without thinking yet?" You can lock it in, or take one more week.

    Lally et al. (2010), European Journal of Social Psychology.

How it works

Step 1 of 6

Start with who.

Pick the person you're becoming. Every habit after this is a vote for them.

Step 2 of 6

Find the cue you never skip.

Pouring coffee, brushing your teeth, getting into bed. These already happen every day, so they make good anchors.

Step 3 of 6

Stack something tiny on top.

After I pour my coffee, I'll put on my running shoes. Keep it small enough that you can't say no.

Step 4 of 6

Now make it easy.

Selfward asks what you can set up ahead of time, so the habit is right in front of you when the cue comes.

Step 5 of 6

Show up. Missed a day? Fine.

Missing one day keeps your run going. Miss two in a row and it starts over. Never miss twice.

Step 6 of 6

Lock it in.

Once it happens without thinking, it comes off your list for good. Then you choose what stacks on top of it next.

What stacking means

One habit becomes the cue for the next.

Start with a routine you already do. Add one tiny habit on top. Once it's automatic, lock it in. It becomes part of the base, and the next habit stacks on top. Over months, a single cup of coffee turns into a whole morning.
